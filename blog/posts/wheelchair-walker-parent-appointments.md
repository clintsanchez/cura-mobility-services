## Helping an elderly parent with a walker or wheelchair get to appointments

:::quick-answer
Plan for extra time, clear a safe path to the car and use the sit-first transfer: your parent backs up until the seat touches their legs, holds the seat or car frame (not the door), sits, then swings both legs in. Bring the walker or wheelchair, and tell the clinic and the driver about mobility needs ahead of time.
:::

:::tldr
- More than one in four older adults fall each year, so the trip itself deserves a plan.
- Clear the path from the door to the car, and use flat shoes with a good grip.
- Use the sit-first car transfer, and never let your parent pull on the car door.
- Lock the wheelchair brakes and move the footrests before any transfer.
- Call the clinic ahead about entrances, parking and wheelchair space.
- Tell the ride service about the walker or wheelchair every time you book.
:::

For a parent who uses a walker or wheelchair, a 20-minute checkup can take half a day. There are steps to handle, a car to get into and out of, a parking lot and a waiting room. ## Why mobility changes the whole trip

Your parent is not the only one using a mobility aid. A 2025 study in the American Journal of Epidemiology found that <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13417826/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">22.2 percent of older adults living in the community used a mobility device</a>, including about 3.8 million who used walkers and 1.9 million who used wheelchairs.

Moving between places is where the risk is. The CDC reports that <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/falls/data-research/facts-stats/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">more than one out of four older people falls each year, but less than half tell their doctor</a>. The CDC also calls <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/falls/data-research/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">falls the leading cause of injury-related death among adults 65 and older</a>.

Doorsteps, curbs, car doors and slick parking lots are all part of an appointment day. That is why the trip needs as much planning as the visit. Coming home after a hospital stay adds its own steps, which we cover in [planning the ride home from the hospital](/caregiver-guides/planning-ride-home-hospital-discharge/).

## Before the appointment: set your parent up to succeed

Most of a smooth trip happens before anyone leaves the house. Start the day before.

Call the clinic and ask a few questions: Which entrance has no steps? Is there a drop-off area near the door? Can they have a wheelchair waiting, or is there room for your parent's own? Some offices have one accessible exam room, so it helps to mention mobility needs when you confirm the time.

At home, clear the path from the door to the car. Move rugs, turn on the porch light for early pickups and check for wet steps. Choose flat, closed shoes with a good grip and loose clothes your parent can sit and stand in easily.

Pack the bag the night before: photo ID, insurance cards, a medication list, glasses, hearing aids and a water bottle. If your parent uses a walker, check that the rubber tips or glides are not worn down.

## Getting into and out of the car safely

The car transfer is the step families worry about most. A simple, repeatable method makes it safer and calmer.

<!-- svg:car-transfer | The sit-first method for getting into a car. Ask your parent's physical therapist about their specific needs. -->

A UW Medicine car transfer guide, written for patients after hip or knee surgery, lays out a method that works well for many older adults. Your parent should <a href="https://healthonline.washington.edu/sites/default/files/record_pdfs/Car-Transfers_a11y.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">back up to the car until the back of their legs touch it, then hold the car frame or seat, not the car door, walker or crutches</a>. They sit first, then pivot and bring their legs in together.

Getting out works in reverse. The same guide says to pivot the legs out without twisting, hold the seat or frame, not the door, and stand up. Set the walker in front of your parent before they rise, not after.

For a wheelchair transfer, MedlinePlus advises that you <a href="https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000428.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">put the brakes on and move the footrests out of the way</a> before your parent stands. Bend your knees and keep your back straight when you help.

If your parent cannot bear weight or the transfer feels unsafe, stop. Ask their doctor or physical therapist for a plan before the next trip.

## At the appointment: parking, check-in and waiting

Arriving is its own set of steps. A few habits make it easier for your parent and for you.

Drop your parent at the entrance before you park, with the walker set up or the wheelchair brakes locked. Never leave them standing alone at a curb. If you are driving alone, use the accessible parking space if your parent has a placard.

Check in for your parent so they can sit down right away. Ask the front desk if there is a quiet corner, or a spot away from the door where the wheelchair is out of the path.

Bring something to do and something to drink. Waits are often longer than expected, and a tired parent is more likely to stumble on the way out.

## When a door-to-door ride makes more sense

Some weeks you cannot drive. Some trips are too hard to manage alone. That is when outside help earns its place.

A door-to-door ride means the driver helps from your parent's front door to the seat, and from the car to the check-in desk. That is different from a curb pickup, where your parent handles the steps and doors alone.

## What caregivers ask about walkers, wheelchairs and appointments

These are the questions families ask most when a parent's mobility starts to change.

### Should my parent bring their own wheelchair to the appointment?

Usually, yes, if the chair fits in the car and your parent is comfortable in it. Their own chair is fitted to them. Some clinics and hospitals keep wheelchairs at the entrance, but availability varies. Call ahead to ask, and tell the ride service if the chair needs to come along.

### How do I help my parent into a car without hurting my back?

Let your parent do as much as they safely can. Stand close, bend your knees and keep your back straight rather than lifting. Have them back up to the seat, hold the seat or car frame and sit first. If you find yourself lifting most of their weight, ask a physical therapist for a safer method.

### What if my parent cannot manage the steps at home?

Talk with their doctor about a home safety check or physical therapy, which can help with steps and transfers. In the meantime, tell anyone driving your parent about the steps before pickup, so they can plan extra time and help. Some families add a sturdy handrail or a portable ramp.

### Can a caregiver ride along to the appointment?

In most cases, yes. Mention it when you book so there is room for you and the mobility aid. Riding along lets you help at check-in, take notes during the visit and ask questions your parent may forget. It also gives your parent a familiar face on a hard day.

### How can I tell if my parent is at higher risk of falling?

Watch for a new shuffle, holding onto furniture to walk, dizziness when standing or a recent fall they did not mention. The CDC notes less than half of older people who fall tell their doctor. Share what you see with their doctor, who can check balance, vision and medications.
